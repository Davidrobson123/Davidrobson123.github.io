    How the simulation works

    The simulations run here in the page, on your phone or computer: pick a game or any matchup, choose how many simulations, and press Run again for a fresh set. Each simulated game gives both teams a number of drives, and each drive ends in a touchdown, a field goal or nothing, at rates set by that offence against that defence, recent games weighted most. Yards follow from how well the drives went, and each team's yards, catches and touchdowns are shared out to players by their recent usage: carries, targets and red-zone work.

    For scheduled games, team scoring is anchored to the betting line, which is more accurate than any team rating we've built so far. The engine's job is everything below team level: who scores, who gets the yards, and how all of it moves together.

    Every simulation uses the rosters and injury reports as of the time shown at the top of the page; players ruled out late on game day may not be included. Players on injured reserve, cut or traded, and anyone listed Out or Doubtful for that week, are left out and their carries and targets go to teammates at the same position. Questionable players stay in, marked Q. Weekly injury reports come out Wednesday to Friday, so Sim Lab is refreshed after the final report.

    Each simulated game starts with a coin toss: the winner defers, the other team receives, and the roles swap at half-time, so the team receiving a half can get the extra drive. Tied games go to overtime under the 2025 rules (both teams get the ball, then sudden death, a tie if still level after ten minutes). Home field is worth about 1.7 points, plus about 0.25 points per day of rest advantage, both measured from betting lines on 2,960 games since 2015. Neutral sites get no home edge.

    Each defence's pattern is used too: if it gives up more catches or touchdowns to tight ends, say, the simulation leans that way for the tight ends it faces, without changing the team's total. Wind over 10 mph cuts passing yards by about 1.3% per mph, field goals by 1.1% and touchdowns by 0.8%, and freezing temperatures cut field goals, all measured on 2024–26 outdoor games. Enter the forecast for outdoor games; indoor games get no adjustment.

    Watch a simulation plays one game out snap by snap: down and distance, the line of scrimmage (blue), the first-down line (yellow) and the game clock. After every play the rest of the game is re-simulated a few hundred times from that moment, which gives the win-probability line. The momentum bars show how far each drive moved it.

    Player numbers are built from opportunities: each simulated game gives a player targets, some become catches and each catch carries its own yards; rushing works the same way through carries. A player with no catch gets no receiving yards. A player's share of the work also varies from game to game (snap counts, game script, early exits), more so for smaller roles, and regulars who are on the field a lot keep a fair chance of being thrown to even if their target count is low.

    When the betting market expects a team to score less than usual, for example with a backup quarterback, its passing and rushing yards drop with it. International games have tended to go under the total, so they get 1 point off it.

    Low – high under each median is a poor game and a big game: 8 in 10 simulated games land between them. The typical game is one box score built from every simulation: each team's median score, the players most likely to score its touchdowns and each player's median line. It is the middle of all the simulations, not a prediction of the exact result.

    Sim Lab shows simulations only. It doesn't recommend bets: the percentages are the sim's view of each outcome, to compare with whatever prices you see.
